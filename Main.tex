\textbf{\textit{Details of oscilloscope probe}}\\

Fig.~\ref{fig:Oscilloscope_probe} shows a typical oscilloscope probe.

\begin{itemize}
\item The Oscilloscope probe is a passive connector which is used to connect oscilloscope to the electrical circuit. It consists of three parts Retractable hook tip (or crocodile clip), a Crocodile clip and BNC connector.
\item BNC connector: It is used to connect oscilloscope to the probe.
\item Retractable Hook tip: It is used to connect the node of the electrical circuit to the oscilloscope where there is a need to measure the signal.
\item Crocodile clip: It is connected to the ground of the electrical circuit in the bread board.
\item Make sure the RED slider on the probe should be on the X1 position only. X1 means amplification is unity.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Sample:}


You are given a resistor whose stripes are colored from left to right as brown, black, orange, gold. Find
the resistance value.
\begin{itemize}
\item The gold stripe is on the right so go to Step Two.
\item The first stripe is brown which has a value of 1. The second stripe is black which has a value of 0.
Therefore the first two digits of the resistance value are 10.
\item The third stripe is orange which means x 1,000.
\item The value of the resistance is found as 10 x 1000 = 10,000 ohms i.e., 10~k$\Omega$ 
\item The gold stripe means the actual value of the resistor may vary by $5\%$ meaning the actual value
will be somewhere between 9,500 ohms and 10,500 ohms. (Since $5\%$ of 10,000 = 0.05 x 10,000 = 500)
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Sample calculation for set 2:}\\
\begin{itemize}
    \item The relation between desired gain and $R_G$ is given by
\begin{equation}\label{desired gain}
    G = 1 + \frac{49.4~k\Omega}{R_G}
\end{equation}
The $R_G$ is the resistor in an operational amplifier that sets the amplifier's overall gain. In this, our desired gain is 5. So from Eq.~\ref{desired gain}, we can calculate $R_G = 12.35k\Omega$.

\item Measured gain (G) is calculated by 
\begin{equation}
    G = \frac{\text{Measured~output}}{\text{Selected~input}}  = \frac{1060}{200} = 5.3
\end{equation}
\end{itemize}

\noindent\textbf{Conclusions/Discussion on the Results:}
\begin{itemize}

\item The operation of the INA 129 instrumentation op-amp was thoroughly observed and analyzed, demonstrating its effectiveness in amplifying small input signals with high precision. 

\item The gain was measured across various resistor values, showing that the experimental results closely matched the theoretical values. 

\item The INA 129 is designed for high-precision applications, offering excellent common-mode rejection and high input impedance, making it ideal for amplifying low-level signals in noisy environments.

\item The input DC voltage significantly impacts the output signal, as it can shift the entire waveform vertically and potentially drive the output into saturation if the DC offset is too high, highlighting the importance of managing DC offsets in practical applications. 
 

\item The gain, determined by the resistor $R_G$ connected between pins 1 and 8, varied with different resistor values, illustrating the versatility of the INA 129 .A measured output of 1.06 mV with $R_G$ at 12.35 k$\Omega$ resulted in a gain of approximately 5.3, closely aligning with the theoretical gain of 5. Similarly, adjusting $R_G$ to 48.4 k$\Omega$ resulted in a lower gain, consistent with expected amplifier behavior. Overall, the measured gains consistently matched the calculated values, confirming the reliability of the INA 129. 

\item This amplifier is particularly suitable for applications requiring high accuracy and low noise, such as medical instrumentation and sensor data acquisition, where preserving signal integrity is critical. 

\item The experiment provided valuable insights into the behavior of instrumentation amplifiers and their role in enhancing signal fidelity in complex electronic systems, suggesting that future investigations could further explore the INA 129's performance under various input types and conditions to assess its robustness and versatility

\end{itemize}

\begin{equation}
    \text{Calculated gain} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+\tau^2\omega^2}} = \frac{1}{\sqrt{1+0.0001^2\times 2513.27^2}} = 0.9698.
\end{equation}

\begin{equation}

\item The cutoff frequency for the RC circuit was calculated .This frequency is the point where the output signal starts to attenuate significantly
\item The measured static gain and phase difference were recorded at various input frequencies. As expected, at frequencies below the cutoff, the circuit showed a minimal reduction in output amplitude, while at higher frequencies, the output signal amplitude decreased as the circuit acted more like a low-pass filter. This behavior was consistent with the theoretical expectations for a first-order low-pass filter.
\item The Bode plot (Figure.~\ref{fig:cutoff}) showed the variation of measured static gain (in dB) with frequency. The plot revealed two asymptotes, with the gain flattening out at lower frequencies and gradually decreasing above the cutoff frequency. The cutoff frequency in the experiment was observed around 1600 Hz, which closely matched the theoretical value of 1591.55 Hz.
\item A comparison of measured and theoretical gain (Figure.~\ref{fig:Gain comparison}) showed that the experimental results were in good agreement with the theoretical model, indicating that the RC circuit behaved as expected for a first-order low-pass filter.
\item The phase difference between the input and output signals was also measured and compared with theoretical predictions (Figure.~\ref{fig:phase comparison}). The phase lag increased with frequency, as expected for a low-pass filter. At lower frequencies, the phase difference was small, while at higher frequencies, the phase difference approached $\pi/2$ radians, which is typical for a first-order system near and beyond the cutoff frequency.
\item The RC circuit’s response to a square wave input was also examined. The output voltage was measured at various frequencies (Table.~\ref{tab:gain for square wave}), showing a reduction in amplitude as the frequency increased. This behavior confirmed the filtering characteristics of the RC circuit, where high-frequency components of the square wave were attenuated, leaving behind a smoother waveform at higher frequencies
\end{itemize}

Both theoretical and experimental results for gain and phase difference showed excellent agreement, indicating that the RC circuit accurately simulates a first-order system. The circuit’s performance as both a low-pass filter (RC Integrator) and a high-pass filter (RC Differentiator) illustrates its versatility in various filtering applications. Overall, the results validated the theoretical principles of first-order RC circuits and their use in filtering applications .This experiment reinforced the importance of RC circuits in signal processing, noise reduction, and frequency filtering

The key objectives were achieved by constructing the circuit on a breadboard, varying the resistance to observe the effect of different damping ratios, and plotting the amplitude ratio versus the frequency ratio.
\item The theoretical resonance frequency is equal to 2250.8 Hz. The experimentally observed resonance frequency, however, was 2600 Hz, deviating by about 15.5\% from the theoretical prediction. This deviation can be attributed to factors such as:
Component tolerances (resistor, capacitor, and inductor) that could vary from their nominal values.
Measurement errors, including inaccuracies from the oscilloscope or the function generator.
Parasitic inductance and capacitance from the breadboard and wiring, which could shift the resonance point slightly.

\item The experimental data showed a typical resonance curve, where the output voltage initially increases with frequency, reaches a peak at the resonance frequency, and then decreases as the frequency continues to increase.
 The voltage peak corresponds to the resonance condition, where the impedance of the RLC circuit is minimized, and maximum energy is transferred from the input signal to the circuit. This was observed clearly in the graph of output voltage versus frequency (Figure.~\ref{fig:Resonant frequency}), where the highest voltage was recorded at 2600 Hz.

\item The frequency response was also analyzed for different damping ratios ($\zeta$ = 0.1, 1 and 2), which are crucial in determining the behavior of the circuit near resonance.
    \begin{itemize}
    \item Underdamped case ($\zeta$ = 0.1): For the underdamped system, the output voltage reaches a sharp peak at resonance and then decays as the frequency increases beyond resonance. This is characteristic of an under damped system, where there is a large oscillatory response.

    \item Critically damped case ($\zeta$ = 1): In the critically damped case, the voltage response shows a much smoother curve, with no sharp resonance peak. The system does not oscillate, and the response decays more steadily after resonance.

    \item Over damped case ($\zeta$ = 2): For the over damped system, the voltage response is significantly smaller and decays rapidly as the frequency increases. There is no pronounced peak, and the system is slow to respond to changes in frequency.
    \end{itemize}
    
\item  These observations align with theoretical predictions, where the damping ratio affects the sharpness and height of the resonance peak. As the damping ratio increases, the system becomes less responsive at resonance, and the peak of the voltage response diminishes.
\item  The measured values were in close agreement with the theoretical values, with some deviations. The deviations were especially noticeable in the critically damped and over damped systems, where the measured output was generally lower than the theoretical values. These discrepancies can be attributed to the non-ideal nature of the components and potential inaccuracies in the damping ratio calculation due to component tolerances.
\end{itemize}
